What Is a Skid Steer and How Does It Work

Published on 2026-10-27 by StoneKrusher Heavy Equipment Team

A skid steer can support many types of construction and landscaping work by changing attachments and adapting to different tasks. One day, the machine may grade soil for a patio, then move pallets, drill fence post holes, or clear brush with a grapple. Its compact size and attachment versatility allow one machine to take on work that might otherwise require several pieces of equipment.
The machine's basic design combines a compact frame, wheels or tracks, lift arms, hydraulic systems, and an attachment mounting system. Skid steering allows the machine to turn by varying the speed or direction of the wheels or tracks on each side, while hydraulic power operates the lift arms and many attachments. Rated operating capacity and attachment characteristics also affect how the machine handles, lifts, and travels during different tasks.
Understanding these basic systems helps operators use the skid steer more effectively and safely. Before starting a job, operators should consider the attachment being used, rated capacity, hydraulic requirements, ground conditions, and available working space. Matching the machine setup to the task supports better control and helps operators work confidently across construction, landscaping, agriculture, and other applications.
Basic Design
A skid steer is built around a compact, rigid frame with the engine positioned at the rear to help balance loads carried at the front. The operator station sits near the center inside a protective cab structure, while the lift arms extend along both sides of the cab to the front attachment. Wheels or rubber tracks are mounted on each side of the machine, with each side driven independently. This side-by-side drive layout allows the operator to control the speed and direction of the left and right sides separately, which gives the skid steer its tight turning ability.
Most skid steers use a hydrostatic drive system. The engine powers hydraulic pumps that send fluid to drive motors on each side of the machine. On many wheeled models, the drive motors transfer power to the wheels through a chain case or similar mechanism. The main components work together as one system: the engine provides power, the frame supports the major components, the drive system moves the machine, the operator station contains the controls and protective systems, and the lift arms and attachment plate handle work at the front.
Wheeled and tracked skid steers are designed for different working conditions. Wheeled models generally perform well on firm surfaces such as pavement, concrete, and compacted gravel, while tracked versions, commonly called compact track loaders, spread their weight over a larger contact area and are better suited to soft soil, mud, and turf. The best choice depends largely on the surfaces and ground conditions where the machine will operate most often.
Key takeaway: A skid steer uses wheels or tracks on both sides of a compact frame, and because the left and right sides can operate at different speeds or directions, the machine can turn within a small area.
How Steering Works
A skid steer changes direction without using conventional front-wheel steering. Instead, it controls the movement of the wheels or tracks on each side independently. This skid-steering system allows the machine to turn within a very small area, making it well suited to confined jobsites.
When both sides move at the same speed and direction, the machine travels straight. During a turn, one side can slow, stop, or reverse while the other continues moving, causing the machine to pivot. When the two sides move in opposite directions, the skid steer can rotate close to its own center. Operators should also understand the machine's control pattern and remember that sharp turns create additional friction and surface wear.

Independent Side Control: Each side of the machine can be controlled separately to change direction.
Differential Movement: Changing the speed or direction between the two sides creates the turning motion.
Tight Turning: Opposite movement between the sides allows the machine to pivot within a small area.
Operator Controls: Joysticks, levers, or other control layouts manage travel and steering depending on the machine.
Surface & Component Wear: Sharp turns can increase tire or track wear and may damage sensitive surfaces such as lawns or finished pavement.
Key takeaway: A skid steer turns by controlling the movement on each side of the machine, so one side slowing down, stopping, or reversing relative to the other creates the turn.
Hydraulic Power Runs Attachments
Hydraulics provide the working power behind a skid steer. The engine drives hydraulic pumps that pressurize fluid and send it to the drive motors, lift arm cylinders, tilt cylinders, and hydraulic attachments. The auxiliary hydraulic circuit carries this flow through hoses along the lift arms to quick couplers at the front of the machine. Once an attachment such as an auger, trencher, grapple, breaker, or sweeper is connected, the auxiliary system supplies the hydraulic power needed to operate its motor or cylinders through controls built into the joystick or operator controls.
Most skid steers offer standard-flow and high-flow auxiliary hydraulics. Standard flow works with many common attachments, while high flow provides greater hydraulic flow for more demanding tools such as cold planers, mulchers, and some trenchers. Attachment compatibility depends on matching the machine's hydraulic flow and pressure with the attachment's requirements. If an attachment needs more flow than the skid steer can provide, it may operate slowly or perform poorly. Operating outside the recommended range can also damage the attachment or hydraulic system, so both sets of specifications should be checked before connecting.
Proper hydraulic maintenance helps protect the entire system. Check the hydraulic fluid level during pre-shift inspections and use only the fluid specified by the manufacturer. Clean auxiliary couplers before making connections to prevent dirt from entering the system, and relieve hydraulic pressure according to the manufacturer's procedure before connecting or disconnecting hoses. Inspect hoses regularly for cracks, abrasion, and leaks. Never use your hand to search for a hydraulic leak because pressurized fluid can penetrate the skin and cause serious injury. Use a piece of cardboard or another safe inspection method instead.
Key takeaway: Hydraulic systems power the lift arms and many attachments, and auxiliary hydraulic flow lets tools such as trenchers, augers, and grapples operate directly from the loader.
Lift Arms Handle Material
The lift-arm system controls the attachment at the front of a skid steer. Two arms run along the sides of the cab and connect to the frame through rear pivot points. Hydraulic lift cylinders raise and lower the arms, while tilt cylinders roll the attachment forward and backward. These movements allow the operator to perform common tasks such as loading, carrying, dumping, grading, digging, and positioning materials. For example, a bucket can be lowered into a pile, curled back to hold material, raised for transport, and tilted forward to dump at the desired location.
Skid steers generally use either radial-lift or vertical-lift arm designs. Radial lift arms travel through an arc as they rise and often provide strong reach at mid-height, making them well suited to digging, grading, and general ground work. Vertical lift arms follow a more upright path and keep the load closer to the machine as they reach full height, which makes them useful for loading trucks and placing palletized materials at elevated positions. Regardless of lift design, operators must stay within the machine's rated operating capacity, which is based on the manufacturer's specified tipping load limits. The attachment itself counts toward that capacity, while dense materials such as wet soil and crushed stone can increase the load quickly.
Safe load handling also depends on how the lift arms are operated. Carry loads as low as practical, raising them only enough to clear the ground and obstacles, then lift them to the required height when reaching the dumping or placement area. Use smooth, controlled movements when handling raised loads, and never walk or work beneath raised lift arms. Before entering or leaving the cab, lower the attachment flat to the ground or secure the lift arms with the manufacturer's approved arm support device.
Key takeaway: The lift-arm system raises and lowers the attachment at the front of the machine, allowing a bucket to scoop and move material while forks and other tools handle different material-handling tasks.
Compact Size Supports Maneuverability
A skid steer's compact footprint is one of its biggest advantages. Many construction, landscaping, and agricultural sites don't have room for full-size loaders. Gates, side yards, barn aisles, interior spaces, and crowded jobsites all limit the size of equipment that can work effectively.
Compact dimensions combined with tight turning capability let a skid steer work where larger loaders have limited access. The machine can move between stockpiles, turn around near structures, and work along fences and walls. On busy sites, it can clean up, load, and stage materials without needing wide turning lanes.
This maneuverability saves time on many tasks. Short loading cycles become faster when the machine can turn quickly between the pile and the truck. Cleanup work goes more smoothly when the operator can reach into corners and tight areas. Interior demolition and renovation work becomes practical when the machine fits through doorways and openings.
Tight spaces still require care. A compact machine reduces clearance problems, but it doesn't remove them. Watch the attachment, the lift arms, and the rear of the machine, which swings outward during a turn. Keep workers on foot clear of the travel and turning area, use a spotter where visibility is limited, and look in the direction of travel before every move. Every skid steer has blind spots, especially to the rear and sides.
Measure the work area and access route before bringing the machine in. Check gate widths, overhead clearances, turning space, and ground conditions. Lay ground protection mats across lawns and finished surfaces where needed, since skid steering can mark or tear soft ground.
Key takeaway: A skid steer's compact footprint and tight turning capability let operators work around structures, stockpiles, and other jobsite obstacles where larger loaders have limited access.
Attachments Expand Its Uses
Attachments make a skid steer one of the most versatile machines on a jobsite, allowing one machine to handle tasks such as digging, grading, lifting, drilling, sweeping, trenching, clearing, and demolition. Common tools include buckets for loading and grading, pallet forks for moving palletized materials, grapples for brush and debris, augers for posts and footings, and trenchers for utilities, irrigation, and drainage. Breakers, sweepers, and other specialized attachments can expand the machine's capabilities even further. Most skid steers use a standardized quick-attach mounting plate that allows attachments to be changed quickly. The attachment frame aligns with the machine's mounting plate, then locking levers, pins, wedges, or a powered locking system secure the connection.

A secure attachment connection is essential before operating or lifting a load. Clean mud and debris from the mounting plate and coupler areas, fully engage the locking mechanism, and visually confirm that both locking pins or wedges have passed through the attachment's slots. Relieve hydraulic pressure before connecting auxiliary hoses, and route the hoses away from pinch points. Every attachment also changes how the skid steer handles because it can add weight, extend the load farther forward, or reduce operator visibility. Before use, confirm that the attachment matches the machine's mounting system, hydraulic flow, and rated operating capacity, and review the attachment's operating instructions.
Key takeaway: With compatible attachments, one skid steer can handle buckets, forks, grapples, trenchers, augers, and other tools, supporting a wide range of construction work.
Conclusion
A skid steer combines a compact frame, independent drive on each side, hydraulic power, lift arms, and attachment compatibility to perform a wide range of construction and material-handling tasks. Wheels or tracks on each side allow the machine to turn by varying the speed and direction of each side, while the hydraulic system powers travel, lift-arm movement, and auxiliary circuits for hydraulic attachments. Lift arms raise, lower, and tilt tools within the machine's rated operating capacity, while the compact footprint supports work in restricted areas. Before operation, operators should check engine oil, coolant, hydraulic fluid, tires or tracks, lift arms, pivot points, cylinders, hoses, couplers, and attachment connections, then confirm attachment compatibility, rated capacity, control settings, and safety systems. Keeping workers clear of the travel and turning area and maintaining the machine according to manufacturer requirements helps support controlled and reliable skid steer performance across different applications.
Frequently Asked Questions
What is a skid steer used for?
A skid steer can handle loading, grading, excavation, material handling, landscaping, site preparation, and many attachment-based tasks. Its compact dimensions and maneuverability make it useful on construction, landscaping, agricultural, and property maintenance sites. The machine's capabilities can be expanded by using compatible attachments suited to the specific job.
How does a skid steer turn?
A skid steer turns by controlling the movement of the wheels or tracks on each side independently. By slowing, stopping, or reversing one side relative to the other, the machine changes direction. This steering method allows tight turns and helps the machine maneuver in confined work areas where conventional steering systems may require more space.
What are auxiliary hydraulics?
Auxiliary hydraulics provide hydraulic flow and pressure to compatible attachments connected to the skid steer. They power tools such as augers, trenchers, grapples, breakers, and other hydraulic attachments. The attachment must match the machine's available hydraulic flow, pressure, connection type, and capacity to operate properly and avoid performance problems.
Why are skid steers versatile?
Skid steers are versatile because their compact design, tight turning capability, and attachment compatibility allow one machine to perform many different tasks. A bucket can handle material, while other attachments can support digging, drilling, grading, demolition, or cleanup. This flexibility can reduce the need for multiple specialized machines on smaller or varied jobsites.
